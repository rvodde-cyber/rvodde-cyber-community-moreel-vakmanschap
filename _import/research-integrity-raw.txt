The Order of Names

Lotte has spent fourteen months on this paper — every experiment, every table, every late night rewriting the discussion section. Her supervisor, Professor Dekker, reads the final draft in silence, then slides it back across the desk. "Put my name first," he says, not looking up. "That's how it works here." She knows the norms: senior authorship signals prestige, secures the next grant, keeps her contract renewal on his desk instead of someone else's. Her name would still appear — third, maybe fourth, buried where hiring committees rarely look. She thinks of the data she collected alone on weekends, the results that are unmistakably hers. Refusing might cost her the postdoc she needs. Signing might cost her the only thing she actually owns. She opens the author list and hovers over the first line. What does she do?

The Extra Name on the List

The manuscript is ready, reviewed, accepted — until an email arrives from the publisher. A senior editor at a partner institution would like to be added to the editorial board for this special issue. He hasn't reviewed a single submission, hasn't attended a meeting, hasn't read the papers. But his university covers the open-access fees for the entire issue, and the contract renewal is due next month. "It's just a name on a page," the managing editor says, not quite meeting anyone's eyes. The actual editors, who spent months reading manuscripts and chasing reviewers, say nothing at first. Refusing might mean losing the funding that keeps the journal free to authors. Agreeing means quietly rewriting who did the work. The deadline for the final editor list is tomorrow at noon. Someone has to reply to the email.

The Same Result, Twice

Dr. Willemsen's dataset from three years ago is solid — peer-reviewed, published, done. But the funding cycle never really closes. She adjusts a variable, reframes the research question slightly, submits it as a new proposal. The committee, unaware it overlaps almost entirely with her last funded project, approves it. She presents the "new" findings at a conference in Lisbon, then again, reworded, in Porto. Nobody who reviews her application sees the earlier version; nobody who applauds at the podium knows either. Each grant funds real work — travel, a research assistant, a few months of breathing room. Nothing about the numbers is invented. Only the frame keeps shifting, just enough to look new. She's already drafting the next resubmission when the funding body's newsletter mentions a tightening of duplicate-submission checks. What happens if someone finally lines the versions up?
